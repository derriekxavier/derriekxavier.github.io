Why a single page is the harder brief

A multi-page report can bury a weak measure behind three strong ones. One page can't. Everything visible here — the rolling average trend, the ABC split, the year-over-year movement, the target attainment — had to work individually and hold together as a set, because a reviewer scoring this page sees all of it at once.

The page

What's on it

  • A metric selector that switches the KPI card and trend line between Revenue YoY %, LFL Revenue YoY %, Net Revenue, and Attainment %
  • A 28-day rolling average trend line, smoothing daily noise out of the revenue series without hiding the underlying shape
  • ABC classification of revenue by class (A: 69.5%, B: 20.4%, C: 10.2% of revenue) — a Pareto-style view of where revenue concentration actually sits
  • Net Revenue vs Target by fiscal year, alongside Revenue YoY % by fiscal year, so growth and attainment are read side by side rather than in isolation

The metric-selector technique

A disconnected table lists the available metrics; a slicer against that table feeds a single measure that switches its calculation with SWITCH against the selected value. One visual, one measure, no duplicated pages per metric — the same pattern I reused and extended in the GulfCart build afterward.

Like-for-like vs headline growth

Headline Revenue YoY sits at 32.2%, but LFL Revenue YoY — stripping out the effect of new stores or channels opened during the period — comes in at 2.0%. Reporting both on the same page, rather than just the flattering headline number, is the difference between a dashboard that looks good and one a finance stakeholder can actually trust.

What I'd extend next

  • Add a drill-through from the ABC classification into product- or SKU-level detail
  • Surface the LFL calculation logic as a visible note on the page rather than only in the measure definition
  • Add a country/region breakdown alongside the existing currency and fiscal-year slicers
